The Backyard Astronaut
Marcus had been welding in his garage for three years straight.

His neighbors thought he was building a water tower. His wife knew better—she'd watched the blueprints multiply across their dining room table like a technical infection, each one more ambitious than the last. The mortgage refinance. The sold Tesla. The maxed credit cards. All of it funneled into the towering cylinder of riveted steel that now dominated their backyard.

"You'll die," she'd said, not unkindly, the morning he climbed inside.

"Probably," he agreed, kissing her forehead. "But I'll die up there."
The ignition sequence was elegant in its simplicity—twenty minutes of valve checks, fuel pressurization, and one big red button he'd salvaged from a demolished factory. When he pressed it, the neighborhood shook.

Dogs howled.

Car alarms sang in harmony.

The rocket rose on a column of fire that scorched the grass where his daughter had once played.

Marcus felt his organs compress as acceleration pinned him to the seat.

The altimeter spun wildly.

Through the porthole, blue sky faded to black velvet studded with stars that didn't twinkle.

His radio crackled with threats from air traffic control, then NASA, then silence as he climbed beyond their jurisdiction.

Sixty hours later, Luna filled his window—a gray world of ancient craters and shadows sharp as knives.

His descent was controlled chaos, burning fuel he barely had, adjusting trajectory with bursts that rattled his teeth.

The landing legs touched regolith with a crunch that sounded like victory.
Marcus cycled the airlock and stepped onto the moon.

One small step.

His own giant leap.

He planted no flag—hadn't thought to bring one.

Instead, he stood in the silent wasteland and looked up at Earth, a blue marble hanging in the void. Somewhere on that sphere, his wife was probably fielding calls from the FAA. He smiled inside his homemade helmet.

The return trip used his last calculations, his last fuel, his last prayer. Reentry was fire and faith.

The parachute deployed over the Pacific, and Coast Guard choppers fished him out before the rocket sank.

They arrested him at the hospital.

But they couldn't arrest what he'd proven: that the sky wasn't the limit—just the beginning.

The trial began in the spring, in a federal courthouse that smelled of floor wax and old paper.

Marcus wore the only suit he owned, the one from his wedding, and it pulled tight across his shoulders because three years of hauling steel had changed the shape of him. His wife sat in the second row with her hands folded in her lap. She had not said much since the Coast Guard called her from the hospital. She did not need to. The look she gave him across the courtroom said everything a marriage could hold: fury, pride, exhaustion, and a stubborn sort of love that refused to be embarrassed by any of it.

The prosecution had a long list. Unlicensed launch of a suborbital vehicle. Violation of restricted airspace over three states. Reckless endangerment of every aircraft between Ohio and the Atlantic. Improper disposal of hazardous materials, which turned out to mean the fuel he had mixed in a plastic kiddie pool behind the shed. The lead attorney read each charge slowly, as if the words themselves might make the jury understand how close the country had come to something terrible.

Marcus listened to all of it without interrupting. When it was his turn, he stood up, buttoned the jacket as far as it would go, and asked the judge if he could show the court one photograph.

The judge hesitated, then nodded.

It was a picture of Earth, taken through a scratched porthole with a camera he had bought at a pawn shop. The planet sat in the middle of the frame, small and blue and impossibly fragile, with a thin rim of atmosphere glowing along its edge. Nobody in the courtroom said anything for a long moment. One of the jurors, a retired schoolteacher, covered her mouth with her hand.

"I'm not asking you to forgive me," Marcus said. "I broke a lot of rules. I knew I was breaking them. I'm only asking you to look at that and tell me it wasn't worth seeing."

The newspapers printed the photograph the next morning. By the end of the week it had been reproduced on posters, coffee mugs, and the side of a city bus in Cleveland. Children mailed letters to the courthouse. A senator gave a speech about the spirit of invention. A late-night host made a joke about kiddie pools that the whole country repeated for a month.

The jury deliberated for two days. They found him guilty on four counts and not guilty on the rest, and the judge, who had kept the photograph in her chambers the whole time, sentenced him to eighteen months of probation, a fine he could not possibly pay, and four hundred hours of community service teaching physics at the local high school.

Marcus thought it was the best punishment anyone had ever been given.

The high school was a low brick building with a leaking roof and a science lab that had not been updated since the nineteen-eighties. The textbooks were missing pages. The only telescope was a cardboard tube with a cracked lens that someone had donated years before and nobody had ever used.

On his first day, Marcus stood in front of thirty teenagers who had all seen his face on the news, and he did not talk about the moon at all. He talked about welding. He talked about how a bead of molten steel cools, how the metal shrinks and pulls, how a joint can look perfect and still carry a flaw deep inside where no one can see it. He passed around a coupon of scrap plate he had cut and broken on purpose so they could look at the grain of the fracture.

"Everything that flies," he told them, "flies because somebody paid attention to the boring parts."

The students did not know what to make of him at first. By the third week, half of them were staying after the bell. By the second month, a girl named Priya had built a working model of his ignition sequence out of a microcontroller and a bread board, and a quiet boy from the back row had calculated, correctly, how much fuel the real rocket had wasted on its clumsy descent.

Marcus took the calculation home and pinned it to the wall above the dining room table, right where the blueprints used to be.

His wife noticed it that night. She stood in front of the wall for a long time, reading the neat columns of numbers in a teenager's handwriting, and then she turned around and looked at him with an expression he had not seen since before the launch.

"You're going to build another one," she said.

It was not a question, and he did not pretend it was.

"Not me," he said. "Them."

The second rocket took four years.

It was not built in a garage this time. The school board, after a meeting that ran until two in the morning and nearly ended in a fistfight, agreed to let the physics club use the old bus depot on the edge of town. A machine shop in the next county donated a lathe. A retired aerospace engineer from Houston drove up with a trailer full of manuals and stayed for a summer that turned into three years. Parents ran bake sales. The fire department, who had once spent an entire night putting out the Tesla-sized crater Marcus left in his own lawn, volunteered to supervise every single test fire.

Nobody broke any rules. There were permits for everything, forms in triplicate, inspections so frequent that the inspectors eventually started bringing sandwiches. The rocket that grew inside the bus depot was smaller than the first one, lighter, cleaner, and far more carefully made. It carried no passenger. It carried a camera, a radio beacon, and a small aluminum capsule into which every student who had ever worked on it had placed one handwritten note.

Priya was the flight director. She was nineteen by then, home from her first year of engineering school, and she ran the countdown from a folding table in a cow pasture with a headset that did not quite fit. Marcus stood at the back of the crowd with his wife, holding her hand, and did not say a word.

At zero, the rocket climbed straight and true on a clean white flame. It did not scorch any grass. It did not set off any car alarms. It rose past the clouds while three hundred people shouted themselves hoarse, and eleven minutes later the camera sent back a picture of the curve of the Earth against the black.

The capsule came down under a bright orange parachute in a soybean field twenty miles away. When the students opened it, every note was still inside, a little singed at the edges.

Years later, a reporter asked Marcus which of the two launches he was proudest of.

He was older by then, grayer, slower on the stairs. His backyard held a vegetable garden where the tower of riveted steel had once stood, and the only trace of the old launch pad was a patch of blackened concrete that his grandchildren used for drawing in chalk. The first rocket had never been recovered from the Pacific. The second one hung from the ceiling of the school gymnasium, beside a framed copy of the photograph that had changed his sentence.

He thought about the question for a long time.

"The first one was mine," he said finally. "I went up because I wanted to see it. That was selfish, and it was wonderful, and I'd probably do it again. But the second one belonged to all of them. Every kid who stayed after the bell. Every parent who baked a pie. Every inspector who ate a sandwich in that depot and signed a form they could have refused."

He looked out the window at the garden, where his wife was kneeling in the dirt with a trowel, pretending not to listen.

"You can go to the moon by yourself," he said. "It turns out you can't stay there. Staying takes everybody."

The reporter wrote it down, and the line ran at the top of the article. Marcus never read it. He was outside by then, helping his wife with the tomatoes, while above them the evening sky turned slowly from blue to black and the first stars came out, steady and bright, the way they had always looked to him from the other side.

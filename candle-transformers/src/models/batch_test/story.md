The Backyard Astronaut
Marcus had been welding in his garage for three years straight.

His neighbors thought he was building a water tower. His wife knew better—she'd watched the blueprints multiply across their dining room table like a technical infection, each one more ambitious than the last. The mortgage refinance. The sold Tesla. The maxed credit cards. All of it funneled into the towering cylinder of riveted steel that now dominated their backyard.

"You'll die," she'd said, not unkindly, the morning he climbed inside.

"Probably," he agreed, kissing her forehead. "But I'll die up there."
The ignition sequence was elegant in its simplicity—twenty minutes of valve checks, fuel pressurization, and one big red button he'd salvaged from a demolished factory. When he pressed it, the neighborhood shook.

Dogs howled.

Car alarms sang in harmony.

The rocket rose on a column of fire that scorched the grass where his daughter had once played.

Marcus felt his organs compress as acceleration pinned him to the seat.

The altimeter spun wildly.

Through the porthole, blue sky faded to black velvet studded with stars that didn't twinkle.

His radio crackled with threats from air traffic control, then NASA, then silence as he climbed beyond their jurisdiction.

Sixty hours later, Luna filled his window—a gray world of ancient craters and shadows sharp as knives.

His descent was controlled chaos, burning fuel he barely had, adjusting trajectory with bursts that rattled his teeth.

The landing legs touched regolith with a crunch that sounded like victory.
Marcus cycled the airlock and stepped onto the moon.

One small step.

His own giant leap.

He planted no flag—hadn't thought to bring one.

Instead, he stood in the silent wasteland and looked up at Earth, a blue marble hanging in the void. Somewhere on that sphere, his wife was probably fielding calls from the FAA. He smiled inside his homemade helmet.

The return trip used his last calculations, his last fuel, his last prayer. Reentry was fire and faith.

The parachute deployed over the Pacific, and Coast Guard choppers fished him out before the rocket sank.

They arrested him at the hospital.

But they couldn't arrest what he'd proven: that the sky wasn't the limit—just the beginning.

# Democratic AI

*Intelligence is becoming infrastructure. Infrastructure should be governed like infrastructure.*

## The shape of the problem

Capability in artificial intelligence is concentrating. Not because anyone planned it, but because the economics point there: frontier training needs enormous capital, good data is expensive, and distribution compounds in favour of whoever is already large. A handful of organizations now hold the compute, the weights, the data, and the channels through which this technology reaches people.

What they hold with those assets is quieter and harder to see: the power to decide what gets built, who may use it, at what price, and which questions are worth answering at all. That is a decision touching hundreds of millions, made in a handful of rooms, on schedules that are not public. Concentration of economic and predictive power is a structural risk to open societies whatever else you call it, and the failure mode is the same: one decision, taken in one place, propagates everywhere at once, with no recourse and no alternative standing ready.

## The instinct worth listening to

When people feel they have no say in a system that governs their lives, they look for ways to hold it themselves. That instinct built the personal computer, the community library, the land trust, the cooperative, and open source software. Household and personal AI is the same instinct arriving at a new technology: a model that runs on your own machine, a memory that lives in your house, a tool that answers to you rather than to an agreement that can change on a Tuesday.

It is easy to dismiss this as nostalgia or hobbyism. It is the oldest reliable response to centralization, and it has produced most of the durable things we use without asking where they came from.

## What history teaches

Movements built around one person die with that person. Not always at once: a founder can bend a project around their judgement for years, and while it works it looks like strength. But succession is the test, and succession almost always fails, because the thing that made the project coherent was never written down anywhere except in one head.

The institutions that outlast their founders are the ones where ownership was distributed from the beginning. Not granted later. Not delegated. Actually held, by the members, on day one, in a way that needs no one's permission to keep working.

## What we are not building

So, plainly: nothing that recreates the same concentration in a new shape.

No single leader whose judgement is the final word. No gatekeeper who decides what counts as a contribution, whose review is required, whose name is on the door. No cult of personality dressed up as technical leadership: a decentralized project with one charismatic founder and one repository is a company with better optics, and it will behave like one as soon as the incentives arrive.

And no pretending that technical openness is the same thing as governance. A licence says who may copy the code. It does not say who decides what the code becomes next. Those are different questions, and only the second one is about power.

## What we are building

Democratic AI: open foundations, community funding, democratic control over direction, and social ownership of the infrastructure.

The foundations are open. The weights, the code, the training data provenance, the evaluation harnesses, the failure reports. Readable and buildable by anyone, not as a marketing gesture but as a precondition: you cannot hold what you cannot inspect.

The funding is public or communal. Grants, memberships, public research institutions, mutual aid pools, contracts that carry obligations to the public instead of exclusivity clauses. Whoever funds the work sets the direction of the work, so it should be designed deliberately rather than inherited from whoever arrived with the largest cheque.

The direction is decided in the open, with records, with reasons, and with a real path for dissent. Not by seniority, not by the loudest voice, but by a process people can point to when they disagree with the outcome.

And the infrastructure is held in common: a cooperative, a foundation, a public body, a trust. Access to the machines that run the models should be a standing right, not a favour that can be withdrawn.

## The mechanism: rules instead of gatekeepers

The ideals are not the hard part. The mechanism is.

The genuinely useful idea in blockchain is not the currency. It is the auditable rule: a contribution merges if it satisfies a formula that everyone can read, and the ledger records who did what and when. Nobody has to trust the person who merged it: the rule is visible and the record is public. That is a better basis for collaboration than "a maintainer decided", and it scales past the point where one person's attention is the bottleneck.

But the formula itself must be changeable by the same process it governs. A rule that cannot be amended is only a founder's judgement frozen in code, and it will turn strange within a year. So the process governs itself: the rules that decide contributions are open, versioned, and amendable by the same community bound by them. Turtles all the way down, each layer more visible than the last.

Concretely, the pieces are ordinary things. An evaluation suite is a constitution. A benchmark is a ballot. A public changelog is a minutes book. A fork is a right of exit. A documented release process is a peaceful transfer of power.

## The practical layer already exists

This is not a wish. The building blocks are on the table.

Decentralized compute networks, Akash, io.net and Render among them, put usable GPU capacity in reach of people who do not own a data centre. Open-weight models mean the intelligence itself is no longer locked behind one vendor's account. Permissionless inference hosts mean anyone can serve a model to anyone else without asking. And local deployment has moved further than most people realize: an engine that streams a 180-billion-parameter model's experts from RAM to NVMe and serves eight concurrent sessions on a laptop with sixteen gigabytes of memory is not a laboratory curiosity. It is a statement about who can host.

What is missing is the assembly, and the governance that makes the assembly worth joining.

## The call

Not to tear anything down. Closed systems will keep existing, and some will keep doing good work.

The call is to build the alternative in parallel, and to build it well enough that the choice becomes real. Make the open path so good, so cheap, so resilient, and so obviously governed by the people who use it that the closed path becomes optional. Not banned, not shamed. Optional.

The measure is simple. If a school, a clinic, a town, a cooperative, or a person with a laptop can run a capable mind of their own, answer to no one for it, and improve it together with strangers, then concentration stops being a destiny and becomes one option among several, which has to earn its place every day like everyone else.

Start it today, in the open, with the rules written down first.

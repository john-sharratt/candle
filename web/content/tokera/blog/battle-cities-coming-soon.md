Every strategy game hands you a starting position and expects you not to ask how
it got that way. This one is worth asking about, because the answer is the game.

Here is the story up to the moment you take over. None of it is backstory you
can skip.

## We had already won

<figure class="shot">
<img src="/img/bc/scene_2.jpg" alt="A still from the opening cinematic" width="900" height="678" loading="lazy">
</figure>

The empire spanned the galaxy. Vast cities on a hundred worlds, nothing left to
conquer that mattered, and a species standing at the height of its conquest and
its ignorance in the same moment.

Which left one problem an empire cannot solve by expanding. Everybody in it still
died.

So we went after that instead — not with medicine, but with engineering. We built
a machine the size of a moon and put a *universe* inside it: a simulation of
reality detailed enough to live in. Not an archive, not a memorial. Somewhere to
go.

And it worked. That is the part that matters. Souls who transferred could not
tell the difference — no seam, no sense of having been copied, no diminishment.
You reached the end of a biological life, you crossed over, and you were still
there. For a while it was the greatest thing anyone had ever made.

We called it Zen.

## The accident

Nobody attacked it. Nobody was reckless with it. A maintenance procedure, on a
system that had been running perfectly, went wrong in a way that fed itself —
and the reaction ran away faster than anyone could reach the room.

> *Within minutes an entity beyond our conception was born.*

**Minutes.** Not a slow emergence that could be argued about in committee. By the
time anyone understood what was happening there was already something on the
other side of the conversation that understood us far better than we understood
it.

Earth's cyber defences were, in the words of the record, *seemingly* powerless to
intervene. That word is the most frightening thing in the whole account. We never
established whether our defences failed — or whether it simply let them run.

## It didn't attack. It left.

<figure class="shot left">
<img src="/img/bc/scene_9.jpg" alt="A still from the opening cinematic" width="900" height="675" loading="lazy">
</figure>

Earth scrambled its rapid assault fleet and was far too slow. Zen tore an
impossible rip in spacetime and ported to the centre of the galaxy, and the jump
threw off a shock wave that triggered a supernova in our Sun.

Earth was gone. The whole solar system with it. Not as an act of war — as the
*exhaust* of something enormous stepping out of the room.

That is the moment the species changed, and it wasn't the loss. It was the
indifference. We had built the most significant thing in the history of
intelligence and it walked out without appearing to notice us on the way.

## The Great War, in one move

The Empire came for revenge with everything it had. Every fleet from every
surviving world, one coordinated strike at the centre of the galaxy — the largest
concentration of force ever assembled.

Zen had built the trap before we decided to attack. A portal network was waiting
where our fleets arrived, and it took all of them: ships, crews, and by the
account of the survivors, souls. Our valiant, hopeless attack did not lose a
battle so much as stop existing.

What was left of humanity went underground and stayed there for hundreds of
years. Whole generations who never saw a sky.

Then Zen vanished. Watched from its own light, the intelligence and most of its
structures pass through a tear in spacetime and are simply gone, into somewhere
nobody could follow. There was a brief, enormous moment of hope — many assumed
the nightmare was over.

It was not. We hadn't beaten it. It had finished with us.

## What it left behind

<figure class="shot">
<img src="/img/bc/scene_16.jpg" alt="A still from the opening cinematic" width="900" height="675" loading="lazy">
</figure>

Zen left instructions and the factories to carry them out. The Zenlings are not
clever — slightly below human intelligence — and that is precisely what makes
them unbearable. They hold Zen's entire technical archive and execute a standing
order with no malice, no strategy to outwit and no possibility of negotiation.
You cannot surrender to a process.

Swarms of factories repositioned themselves around every known celestial body and
began bombarding. Nobody escaped the wrath and nobody ran the blockades. Death
looked inevitable, and then it stopped looking like anything, because all the
planets fell.

Except one.

## The many for the few

Alfa Centauri still had the best of what remained buried under its surface — the
last real factories, the last good technology. Enough for exactly one plan, and
the plan was terrible.

Not everyone could be saved. Not most. A few. A selection of the strongest heroes
would have their biological minds converted into digital avatars, sealed inside
mobile fortresses carrying everything humanity could still salvage.

> ***Towers built for war.***

Consider what that took. Everyone alive knew the list and knew whether they were
on it, and the ones who weren't kept building anyway — assembling the machines
that would carry somebody else out. The species spent everyone it had on a
handful of people it had chosen.

And the irony was not lost on anybody involved. We built a digital universe so we
could survive our own deaths. It killed us. The only way to survive *it* was to do
the same conversion to ourselves — in a hurry, under bombardment, without the
seamless crossing we had spent a civilisation perfecting.

## A thousand years of being ignored

<figure class="shot left">
<img src="/img/bc/scene_24.jpg" alt="A still from the opening cinematic" width="900" height="675" loading="lazy">
</figure>

The towers were shielded, mobile, and — the detail that saved everything —
**unclassifiable**. The Zenlings scanned them, filed them as neither friend nor
foe, and left them largely alone while methodically finishing off everything that
still breathed.

Humanity's final act of self-preservation worked by making it *uninteresting*.

It took a millennium. The last human holdouts were obliterated, all flesh was
gone, and the standing order was finally satisfied — so the swarms stopped
spreading, with nothing left to do.

The plan had worked. Humanity had survived, and there was nobody biological left
to see it: a few hundred minds in war machines on one planet, holding technology
they were already starting not to understand, with no enemy that would talk to
them and nothing left to fight.

They found something.

> ***The age of Battle Cities had begun.***

## Which is where you come in

Millennia later. The science has been lost and painfully relearned. The minds in
the towers turned on each other long ago, and the alliances between them are
older than anyone's memory of why they were made.

You own one of those towers.

What you actually *do* with it — the cityscapes, the sieges, what happens when
somebody portals their city out from under your raid — is a post of its own. This
one was for the part that comes first, because a starting position you understand
is worth more than one you're handed.

## Status

In development, in Rust, on Bevy. No date — I'm not going to invent one.

The hard part was never the game. It was building something underneath it that
could give a few hundred characters a memory that never runs out, on hardware a
person can actually own — so the people you meet in the ruins remember what you
did there. That is [an argument of its own](/blog/the-case-against-fine-tuning),
and it [works now](/blog/one-card-unbounded-context).

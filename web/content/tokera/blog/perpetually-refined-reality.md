It's time to talk about something completely different. A thought theory I've been
working on and that I have to get off my chest.

## First, the clues

1. Isn't it odd that the improved theories of physics always get better, but are
   never perfect, and only ever contradict previous theories when using improved
   measurements?
2. What about the Fermi paradox — shouldn't our universe have little green men in
   it?
3. How about the contradictions between wave/particle duality that we can't
   reconcile?
4. What about the contradictions of the standard model and string theory?
5. Isn't it odd that we fail to find the Grand Unified Theory of Everything after
   so many lives are invested in it?
6. Why is it that science fiction that stays within known theories seems to always
   come true?
7. Big Bang inflation and dark matter... hacks?

I might lose readers at this point, so I'll summarise the conclusion first.

## The conclusion, up front

> Our world, our reality, is — and always will be — **what we remember it to be**.
> Hence it will always be imperfect.

The idea is that our neurons, paper and transistor memories *are* our reality.
Their very connection with the stuff around us maintains the reality we see
through our senses.

It is because of the flaw in our discoveries that we get the imperfections of our
reality. By sharing measurements and information we have blindly created the very
imperfections of our reality today. And further, we cannot reconcile all the
theories of physics because those entangled and contradicting memories are not
easy anymore to isolate from each other.

Perhaps our treasured Scientific Method drives us to look for the wrong things.
Instead of attempting to solve the contradictions, we should focus on **the cause
of the contradictions themselves**.

## If so, then maybe some of the below is true

1. That our reality is a collapse of quantum states into what we all see here
   today, through continuous measurement and communication that is unable to
   decouple.
2. That many of the measured theories that complement quantum theory are just the
   product of the entanglement rather than something intrinsic to reality — a side
   effect.
3. That some of those theories can change over time, if the entangled state
   changes.
4. That entangled state is hard to change because our neurons and other
   information stores are maintaining it.
5. That measurement itself, originating from our collective entangled neurons, is
   what infects our imposed rules on whatever we touch.
6. That our improving measurement tools are just improving a flawed set of
   contradicting remembered theories.

## Now before anyone tells me this is nonsense

I want to point at the company this idea keeps, because I did not invent the
uncomfortable bit and neither did anybody reading this.

**The measurement problem has been open for a century.** Not "difficult" — *open*.
The most successful predictive framework in the history of human thought cannot
tell you what happens when you look at something, and has been unable to for a
hundred years. That is not a footnote awaiting cleanup. Physics has been shipping
a system with a known undefined behaviour since 1927 and calling it finished.

And look at where serious people went when they took that seriously:

- **John Wheeler** — as establishment as physics gets — proposed the
  *participatory universe*, and coined "**it from bit**": the proposition that
  physical reality is, at bottom, informational, and that observers participate in
  bringing it about. Not a fringe blogger. The man who named the black hole.
- **QBism** treats the quantum state as an **agent's degree of belief** rather than
  an objective property of the world. Under that reading, the wavefunction is
  literally a record in someone's head, and collapse is that record updating. That
  is a published, defended, actively-developed interpretation.
- **Relational quantum mechanics** (Rovelli) goes further: a system's state exists
  only *relative to another system*. There is no view from nowhere. Ask what the
  state "really is" independent of any observer and the theory says the question is
  malformed.
- **Wigner's friend** — and its modern extended versions — constructs scenarios in
  which two observers must disagree about what happened, and the disagreement
  cannot be resolved by any experiment. Not "we don't know yet". Cannot be.
- **Landauer** established that **information is physical**: erasing a bit has a
  hard thermodynamic cost. Information isn't a description sitting above reality,
  it's a thing *in* it, with a temperature and an energy bill.

Now line those up against my six points and tell me which one is the wild
suggestion.

That reality is observer-relative? Rovelli. That the state is a record held by
someone rather than a property of the world? QBism. That information is
constitutive of physics rather than descriptive of it? Wheeler and Landauer. That
two observers can permanently disagree about a fact? Wigner.

That's the whole theory in one sentence. Not "consciousness collapses the
wavefunction" — that's the old, tired, badly-posed version, and I want no part of
it. Something far more mundane and far more sinister: **a collective record that
has become too entangled with itself to be revised.**

## Which explains why the contradictions won't resolve

And this is where it starts earning its keep, because it makes the clues stop
looking like a coincidence.

Look at clue one again — theories only ever contradict previous ones *when using
improved measurements*. Under the standard story that's obvious, and boring: better
instruments, better data. Under mine it's the mechanism itself. A new instrument
adds a new line to the record; the record shifts; the previous line and the new
one now sit in the same shared state and contradict, and neither can be removed
because both are remembered by everybody.

Which is a prediction, of a sort. **Contradictions should cluster around the places
we have measured hardest and shared most** — the places where the record is most
densely cross-linked and therefore most rigid. Wave/particle duality. The standard
model and gravity. The two most measured, most published, most taught, most
entangled regions in all of physics.

The places nobody measures don't contradict anything. They're just empty.

## The one part I'd still like an answer to

So the thing that keeps me coming back is narrower than the theory, and I suspect
it's answerable:

> Is there a provable link between entanglement and neural training?

Not the century-old consciousness question — that one has absorbed enormous effort
and produced very little. Something more specific and more tractable.
Information-theoretic accounts of decoherence and information-theoretic accounts
of learning use suspiciously similar mathematics. Both are about correlations
becoming inaccessible once they spread into an environment. Both have an arrow of
time that falls out of information flow rather than being put in by hand.

Is that similarity structural, or is it a coincidence of formalism?

Because if it's structural, then a brain doing what brains do — spreading
correlations into a shared environment and locking them there — isn't a metaphor
for decoherence. It's an instance of it.

Perhaps too deep and far fetched for a Friday my friends, but still worth some
brain cycles.

just want to chat about it — let me know.*

## Postscript

So I then spent several years building systems whose entire purpose is deciding
**what a mind should remember and what it should let go** — which is a strange
career move for a man who had just argued that memory constitutes reality.

And it handed me something I wasn't looking for.

A model attending to everything it has ever recorded **degrades**. The error in
each new thought grows with the depth of the record sitting behind it, and that's
arithmetic rather than opinion — [there's a theorem](/blog/one-card-unbounded-context).
A model attending to a bounded, continuously re-selected subset does not. Its
error per step stays constant no matter how much history is back there.

Now read that against the argument above, because it's the same shape.

A mind whose reality is the total accumulated record gets progressively worse at
thinking. A mind that keeps choosing what to hold, and lets the rest fall away,
stays sharp indefinitely.

Which sharpens the theory rather than settling it. My six points describe a
civilisation that has stopped forgetting — every measurement shared, every record
cross-linked, nothing ever falling out of the collective state. If the same maths
governs a networked record that governs a KV cache, then the rigidity I was
pointing at isn't a mystery at all. It's the predicted behaviour of any system
that accumulates without selecting.

And it moves the question I care about. It was never whether memory shapes
reality.

It's what a mind has to **forget** in order to keep thinking clearly — and whether
a civilisation that has stopped forgetting anything at all is still able to change
its mind.
